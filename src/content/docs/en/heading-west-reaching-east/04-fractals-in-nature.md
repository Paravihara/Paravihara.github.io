At first glance, fractals may not seem particularly special beyond self-similarity. But deeper investigation reveals some counterintuitive properties. For instance, Benoit Mandelbrot, the founder of modern fractal geometry, once published a paper titled “How Long Is the Coastline of Britain?” His conclusion: the coastline of Britain is infinitely long. From the perspective of classical geometry, this seems nonsensical, defying everyday intuition. Let’s see how this conclusion arises.

Mandelbrot pointed out that many natural phenomena, such as the British coastline, are fractal: no matter how much you zoom in, you see similar curvy patterns. As the measurement scale becomes finer, the measured length increases. If you could measure with infinite precision, the coastline would be infinitely long.

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image40.png)

Of course, there may be limits to how fine a scale we can use—perhaps at the quantum level, we cannot go below the Planck scale. Still, measuring with a very fine scale yields a length many times larger than conventional estimates.

Another classic example is the Koch curve. The Koch curve is defined as follows: “Start with an equilateral triangle of side length 1. On the middle third of each side, attach a smaller equilateral triangle with side length one-third, resulting in a hexagram. Repeat the process on each side of the hexagram: attach an even smaller triangle on the middle third of each side, and continue indefinitely. The shape becomes increasingly intricate, approximating an idealized snowflake.”

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image41.jpeg)

Although the Koch curve is a one-dimensional closed curve that never intersects itself, its fractal structure gives it infinite length. This phenomenon—finiteness containing infinity—is quite striking to those of us raised on classical geometry. It makes us wonder: is what we call “finite” truly finite, or is it an illusion created by our senses and minds?

Due to such unconventional properties, fractal geometry introduces the concept of fractal dimension. For example, the Koch curve has a fractal dimension of about 1.26. From this dimensional perspective, the Koch curve is not strictly one-dimensional, which is why it can be infinitely long within a finite area. Fractal dimensions can be fractional, challenging traditional notions of points, lines, surfaces, and volumes. A curve with dimension 1.9 resembles a surface, while a surface with dimension 2.9 looks almost three-dimensional.

Why are fractal structures so common in nature? How do they arise? Research shows that fractals often result from the repeated iteration of microscopic chaotic mechanisms. For instance, the logistic map we discussed earlier, when iterated repeatedly, produces the bifurcation diagram—a fractal pattern. Before fractal algorithms were developed, generating realistic computer graphics of clouds, rivers, oceans, mountains, and trees was extremely difficult. Today, computers can easily create lifelike natural landscapes thanks to chaos and fractal algorithms. After admiring Hollywood special effects and the wondrous Mandelbrot set, we might pause to reflect: is the reentrant feedback mechanism behind chaos and fractals truly a fundamental principle of nature, including ourselves?

The donut feedback model of second-order cybernetics, after incorporating chaos and fractals, still leaves some questions unanswered, but it begins to look more plausible. At least now we know that simple feedback mechanisms, repeatedly applied, can generate endless complex phenomena. These complex phenomena may appear random and unpredictable, yet they can arise from simple underlying rules. Moreover, they exhibit multiscale self-similarity, just as we see in nature.

What does the following image depict? A neural network in the brain? A distribution of galaxies in the universe (including dark matter and dark energy)? Or quantum foam (spacetime foam) in a vacuum?

The answer: all of the above, because they look strikingly similar. This self-similarity from the microscopic “vacuum” to the human brain’s neural network to the cosmic scale is astonishing. From earlier discussions on neural networks, we know that many functions of our senses and mind arise from simple microscopic mechanisms leading to emergent phenomena in macroscopic networks. Combined with the self-similarity of the universe, we must question how reliable our intuitive understanding of the cosmos and life really is. Are we truly independent individuals living in a vast material universe alongside other individuals? Or, as Eastern wisdom suggests, do all things share a common essence, with individuality being merely an illusion?
