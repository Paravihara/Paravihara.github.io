*August 2nd, 2023*

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image2.png)

These two older articles (part 1 and part 2) analyzing the visual system are revised versions of lecture notes from a science and Buddhism seminar nine years ago. With slight revisions, they serve well as popular science introductions and a guide to second order cybernetics, likely more engaging and readable than the original paper “On Constructing a Reality”.

The Buddha taught that the suffering of life originates from unwise grasping, which in turn stems from erroneous perceptions of the body, mind and the world. Common misconceptions like "Materialism," "Idealism," and "Existentialism" are supported by deeply ingrained theoretical systems and intuitive experiences, making them extremely difficult to dismantle. Traditional methods may require many years of dedicated study, contemplation, and practice of the Dhamma, which for most people is neither accessible nor interesting. However, leveraging cutting-edge discoveries from Western cognitive science to deeply analyze the detailed structure and operating principles of our sensory systems offers a shortcut to dispel these illusions and to enter the path of liberation. This also allows one to appreciate that the Four Noble Truths and Dependent Origination revealed by the Buddha are not religious dogmas or metaphysical theories, but the operational laws governing the body, the mind, the physical world and the suffering and happiness of life.

The article is lengthy, with content that is broad, deep, and subtle, requiring a calm mind for slow contemplation and investigation to be effective. The key breakthrough lies in seeing that the visual system is less like a camera and more like a projector.

## The Magical Vision (Part 1)

There is a Chinese saying: "Hearing is unreliable, seeing is believing." This illustrates how confident people are in the reality of what they see. If someone claimed that the world we see is actually content projected by our visual system, most would likely consider it nonsense. Our intuition tells us that the world we see exists objectively. Whether we look at it or not, it remains as the way we saw it. Even practitioners who believe the Diamond Sutra's teaching that "all phenomena are illusory" can still be easily influenced by what they see because vision is simply too "close" to us. The moment we open our eyes, before any thought arises, a colorful world is already presented before us.

So, what is the essence of vision? Why is such a seemingly real visual experience called the "illusory aggregate of form" by the Buddha? As the saying goes, "Stones from other hills may overshadow the jade of this one." Let's begin with some research findings from Western science to analyze this "magical" phenomenon of vision.

![Figure 1. The Visual Nervous System](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image3.png)

*Figure 1. The Visual Nervous System*

Mainstream Western science posits that vision is a function of the physical nervous system. This system includes a series of components from the eyeballs (the two spheres in the diagram) to the optic nerves, and then to the visual cortex in the brain (V1~V4). The eyeballs are responsible for sensing light and forming images of external phenomena, converting light signals into electrical signals via neurons on the retina. The converted electrical signals are transmitted via the optic nerves to the visual cortex at the back of the brain, where neural networks process the information layer by layer, ultimately constructing the visual image we perceive.

In other words, traditional science believes the operating principle of vision is very similar to that of a camera. However, "the devil is in the details." When we delve deeper into this visual processing, the absurdity of the "camera" model gradually becomes apparent.

![Figure 2. The Three-Layer Structure of the Retina (light enters from the right)](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image4.png)

*Figure 2. The Three-Layer Structure of the Retina (light enters from the right)*

The retina, located at the back of the eyeball, is also called the peripheral brain. In terms of origin, it is the same as the brain and is the part in direct contact with the external world. After light hits the retina, it is converted into electrical signals through the collaboration of three layers of neurons.

1. The first layer is the photoreceptor layer (far left in the diagram), specialized for light sensing. It includes cone cells and rod cells. The human retina has approximately 110-130 million rod cells and 6-7 million cone cells. Rod cells are mainly located in areas of the retina farther from the fovea (the region corresponding to the center of vision), while cone cells are most concentrated in the fovea.

2. The second layer consists of bipolar cells, responsible for connection. About 10 to several hundred photoreceptor cells connect to one ganglion cell in the third layer via bipolar cells.

3. The third layer is the ganglion cell layer, specialized for conducting electrical signals, numbering around 1.2 million. Compared to the over 100 million photoreceptor cells, the number of ganglion cells is significantly reduced. This shows that neurons in the retina already perform a great deal of "neural computation" on incoming external signals, with the primary function of this computation being edge contrast enhancement. We will skip the specific "algorithmic" details of edge extraction here.

The signals processed by the retina are transmitted via two long optic nerve tracts to the visual cortex at the back of the brain. The human primary visual cortex, V1, macroscopically preserves the two-dimensional topological structure (i.e., the geometric relationships) of the retinal image information, albeit with geometric distortion. The cortical area corresponding to the foveal region (the area we perceive as the center of vision) is relatively large.

![Figure 3. Schematic Diagram of the Microanatomy of the V1 Layer of the Visual Cortex](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image5.png)

*Figure 3. Schematic Diagram of the Microanatomy of the V1 Layer of the Visual Cortex*

At the microscopic level, neurons in the V1 layer begin to exhibit complex functional specialization in information processing. The figure above is a schematic of the microanatomy of V1. The size of one functional hypercolumn is about 0.5 to 1 square millimeter. You can think of a functional column as a small grid on the cerebral cortex, with each small grid (functional column) having a similar function. The size of the V1 area corresponding to the central 2 degrees of the visual field is about 2100 square millimeters, containing thousands of functional columns. Neurons within a V1 functional column have the following specializations:

· Blobs: Neurons in this area encode large-scale surface texture information (low spatial resolution).

· Areas around blobs: Neurons encode edge information (high spatial resolution).

· Ocular dominance columns: Areas specializing in input from the left or right eye.

· Orientation columns: Neurons encode (detect) edges with different orientations; neurons in different areas are sensitive to edge signals of specific orientations.

In other words, V1 has already begun extracting information such as texture, edges, and orientation from the retinal image, then sends this information to processing layers V2, V3, and V4. In these subsequent visual cortical layers, information like edges is further processed—for example, edges are combined, overlaid with different texture information, and recognized as different objects, etc. The detailed processing is extremely complex, and we will not delve further.

Every time we open our eyes and see the colorful world, the complex brain signal processing described above is completed automatically and instantaneously. If we calm down, open our eyes, and carefully contemplate this complex process... perhaps we can temporarily step out of the intuition that "seeing is believing" and begin to doubt: Is the scene before our eyes the true appearance of the world? How much of this vivid picture comes from the external world, and how much comes from the information processing of our eyes and brain?

Let's look at another image of the retina:

![Figure 4. Planar Image of the Retina](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image6.png)

*Figure 4. Planar Image of the Retina*

The distribution of photoreceptor cells on the retina is very uneven. The central foveal region has a very high density of neurons, while other parts are much sparser. Also, the point where the axons of ganglion cells, responsible for transmitting electrical signals, penetrate the eyeball to extend toward the brain is called the blind spot, because there are no photoreceptor cells or light-sensing capability there. Furthermore, the retina is covered with numerous blood vessels, and these locations also lack light-sensing capability.

These characteristics cause the image projected onto the retina to undergo various distortions and occlusions. Strangely, we seem completely unaware of these occlusions and distortions! Clearly, the operating principle of the human visual system is not quite like a camera's. If dust or debris falls on a camera's sensor, the resulting photo will definitely show these blocked patches. Yet, despite such severe occlusion and distortion on our retina, we have no perception of it at all! What exactly is going on?

Let's look at an interesting experiment concerning the visual blind spot.

What did you "see" in your blind spot?

When the black dot on the right enters the blind spot, we don't see a black or white hole. What we see is a kind of "filling-in" effect, as if the brain, based on the visual information surrounding the blind spot, helps fill in its content. Some scientists believe the brain isn't actually filling anything in here; the area corresponding to the blind spot simply has no visual content. And this feeling of having nothing is not the all-black, all-white, or empty nothingness we typically understand. It is a kind of imperceptible "sensation," a kind of "presence" beyond having or not having. (Friends familiar with Zen might contemplate whether this "blind spot" and Linji's "True Man of No Rank" are one or two!)

Stubborn defenders of materialism might explain the blind spot experiment above like this: This filling-in by the brain should also be a function evolved through biology. Although the external scene we see is incomplete and has undergone some brain repair, it is still a relatively true reflection of external objects. Meanwhile, some more open-minded Western researchers have begun exploring another possibility: perhaps the world we see is a kind of "illusion" created by the brain.

After all, from a purely scientific perspective, if we provide the human brain with all the electrical signals it needs, it would be hard-pressed to distinguish whether it is living in a virtual reality or a real world. This is also why the movie The Matrix sparked a wave of discussion in American philosophical and religious circles. The main function of the Matrix in the movie is to provide humans with a virtual reality living environment, and the vast majority of humans living within it are completely unaware. Since Plato's time, Western philosophers have been pondering whether objective reality truly exists.

Of course, walking out of the cinema, we can all realize that the likelihood of the entire human race being kept as batteries in a large virtual reality system constructed by robots is slim. But the nature of vision becomes even more perplexing. Neither the materialist view of "seeing is believing" nor the constructivist view of "virtual reality" answers the most fundamental question.

When we lift the veil of the visual nervous system, all we see are some neurons and electrical signals. Although these electrical signals carry the information of the visual images we see, they involve massive distortions, transformations, losses, and fillings-in. Many researchers of the visual nervous system, after clarifying these complex processing flows of electrical signals, still cannot answer why the processing of these electrical signals can give rise to the visual experience we perceive.

In other words, if we build a photoelectric signal processing system using machines to mimic human eyes and brains, would that machine also be able to see? What is the essence of awareness? This is a fundamental question puzzling Western scientists and is where the true "magic" of vision lies.

## The Magical Vision (Part 2)

Last time, we mentioned that Western scientists, after deeply analyzing the visual nervous system, still cannot explain why this nervous system can produce the vision we perceive. In the eyes of Western scientists, the visual nervous system looks like a complex photoelectric processing device. If this device can produce vision, then a robot with similar processing capabilities should also be able to see. But this contradicts the common sense and intuition of most people, and even AI scientists with a positive view find it difficult to verify this issue.

Traditional Western science is built on the principle of objectivism, which requires that the conclusions scientists reach do not depend on the scientists themselves: a theory derived by one scientist must be independently verifiable by other scientists. Such an objective research method effectively prevents potential personal biases of researchers and has led to tremendous success for humanity in natural sciences like physics, chemistry, and mathematics. But any method has its potential issues; even the mighty Achilles had a vulnerable heel. When scientists shift their research object to human cognition, the problems with the objectivity principle begin to surface.

The objects of natural science research are the "objectively existing world," so whether it's the law of universal gravitation or the profound theories of general relativity and quantum physics, corresponding experiments can be designed for verification. But when we encounter questions like "Can a machine also have vision?", experiment design becomes a challenge because the subject of the experience has changed. Although science repeatedly emphasizes objectivity, it still relies on the premise that the subject is human. Otherwise, even designing experiments becomes difficult.

The British mathematician Alan Turing, known as the "father of artificial intelligence," once painstakingly designed an experiment for a similar AI problem, the famous Turing Test:

To test whether a machine possesses human intelligence, an evaluator is separated from a human subject and a machine subject. Through some device (like a keyboard), the evaluator asks questions to both test subjects. After asking a series of questions, if the evaluator cannot reliably tell which responses come from the human and which from the machine, then the machine is said to have passed the Turing Test and is considered to have human intelligence.

We can see that after going in a big circle, this experiment shifts the judging subject back to the human. The dependency of the objective scientific method on human subjectivity is indeed somewhat ironic. In fact, the "objectivity principle" is not exclusive to scientists; it originates from ordinary people's (erroneous) intuitions, so many Dhamma practitioners also frequently fall into similar traps. When you feel you have seen or grasped the ultimate reality or truth, a reality/truth that exists independently and does not rely on anyone, the blind spot of the "objectivity principle" is already secretly at work.

Let's return to our topic. This discussion still revolves around the essence of vision. However, this time we will leave the main road of modern science and enter a rugged, bumpy side path. The destination is a non-mainstream yet highly forward-thinking and insightful cognitive theory: Second-Order Cybernetics.

Readers who have read Kevin Kelly's book “Out of Control” are familiar with the term Cybernetics. Cybernetics was the outcome of a series of interdisciplinary seminars in the 1940s, with the main theoretical founder being the American mathematician Norbert Wiener. The word "Cybernetics" originates from ancient Greek, originally meaning "steersman," later extended by Plato to mean "effective governance"—a very cool name. Unfortunately, the Chinese translation of Cybernetics, "控制论" (literally control theory), is a very rigid and easily off-putting term. People love freedom, so who likes to be controlled?

In his foundational 1948 work, Wiener defined cybernetics as: the theory of control and communication in animals and machines. The focus on animals is a major distinction between cybernetics and control engineering. As the foundational theory of systems science, the object of cybernetics is systems. It is less concerned with questions like what components a system has, the properties of each part, or the relationships between parts, etc.—those are the so-called "reductionist" scientific research approaches.

Cybernetics is always concerned with the state in which the overall system exists. Starting from this perspective, it established a rigorous mathematical system that can be used to explain the behavior of almost all complex systems. This is why cybernetics is a pioneering field in computer science and artificial intelligence, and why it can be widely applied in fields beyond engineering, such as psychology, sociology, ecology, chemistry, and brain science.

Second-Order Cybernetics is a school formed by some cybernetics scientists in the 1970s. The systemic view of cybernetics is already very different from the reductionist view of classical science. Moreover, theories like self-organization and attractors in classical cybernetics have begun to overturn many basic perceptions of classical science about the objective world. Second-order cybernetics goes even further. Scholars of second-order cybernetics believe there is no objective reality independent of the observer. Therefore, a theory describing the "objective world" can only be complete if it also includes the observer as an object of study. This indeed sounds a bit mind-boggling: a theory proposed by a scientist must not only explain external phenomena but also explain how the scientist themselves came up with the theory. So, it can also be said that second-order cybernetics is cybernetics applied to cybernetics itself. This logical paradox makes many scientists shy away. Even for ordinary people, asking them to consider themselves along with the problem when thinking is a headache.

One of the most heretical views of second-order cybernetics is: the "objective reality" we perceive is created by the brain, i.e., so-called Constructivism. The founder of second-order cybernetics, Heinz von Foerster, has a paper titled "On Constructing a Reality" specifically discussing this reality-creation process. Let's try to understand the main content of this paper together:

The paper begins by citing the blind spot example we mentioned in the previous article, pointing out the strange phenomenon of our unawareness of the blind spot's existence. It then cites a strange phenomenon experienced by a patient whose visual cortex was damaged by trauma. Weeks after the injury, the patient experienced loss of motor control on one side of their body. Examination revealed the muscles worked fine; the real cause was the disappearance of vision on that side. Strangely, the patient themselves was completely unaware of the loss of vision on one side, just as we are completely unaware of our own blind spot. (What is the reason for this phenomenon?) In this case, the treatment for the loss of motor control is simple: cover the patient's eyes for one to two weeks, allowing the perception of limb position to shift from vision to proprioception.

Next, the author describes another auditory experiment: A word is recorded on a tape and played back in a continuous loop. After a minute or two, most people experience "auditory hallucinations," hearing phrases and words that weren't originally there. The text lists some of the "hallucinated" content heard by two hundred people after repeated playback of the word "cogitate": agitate; annotate; arbitrate; artistry; back and forth; brevity; ca d'etait; candidate; can't you see; can't you stay; cape cod you say; card estate; cardio tape; car district; catch a tape; cavitate; cha cha che; cogitate; computate; conjugate; conscious state; counter tape; count to ten; count to three; count yer tape; cut the steak; entity; fantasy; God to take; God you say; got a date; got your pay; got your tape; gratitude; gravity; guard the tit;

(What is the reason for this phenomenon?)

Next, Heinz describes a cat hearing experiment. A cat with probes implanted in its auditory cortex is placed in a cage with a food box. The food box can be opened by a lever, but the lever only works when a 1000Hz beeping sound is activated. Initially, this cat doesn't know the relationship between the beep, the lever, and the food. The first line of signal represents the external beep, and the following eight lines represent eight progressively deeper locations in the cat's auditory cortex.

It can be seen that when the cat doesn't understand the meaning of the beep, its auditory cortex is basically in a state of noise (a chaotic state?). When it understands that the beep means food, the auditory cortex exhibits a neural response completely consistent with the beep. Being deaf to sounds it doesn't care about (doesn't understand), no wonder some say cats are great Chan masters!

In the above experiments, we see that organisms can sometimes see or hear things that don't exist, and sometimes be blind or deaf to things that do exist. From the cat's example, what ultimately allows the cat to hear the beep is its understanding of it. We can infer that the world the cat experiences changes due to changes in its understanding. The key question now is: Is this experiential change subjective or objective? In other words, does objective reality exist? (Please pause to consider this question before continuing.)

Many people would likely answer affirmatively: Of course objective reality exists. That 1000Hz beep is obviously objectively present; it's just that the cat didn't understand its meaning, so it was deaf to it. From the scientific spirit of second-order cybernetics, those holding this view are suspected of cheating. Heinz might ask: Who is it that says that 1000Hz beep is objectively present? What are the operating principles of that person's own perceptual system? If their perceptual system's principles are the same as the cat's, then wouldn’t the reason for them hearing this beep be due to their brains have completed the relevant training? If we include all observers, including the scientist themselves, in the content being studied, would we still be so sure that an objective sound exists?

Here, we must applaud some pioneers of second-order cybernetics! Although the philosophical view that subject and object are inseparable has a long history, in the Western scientific community where the spirit of objectivism is revered as sacred, it is truly commendable that they were willing to risk their scientific reputation, status (and funding) to courageously question the pitfalls of objectivism. To use an imperfect analogy, second-order cybernetics feels a bit like the Gnostics, Sufis, Mahamudra, or Zen within the scientific community.

As a scientific theory, second-order cybernetics obviously cannot remain at the level of philosophical speculation. After breaking free from the constraints of pure objectivism in science, second-order cybernetics scholars still face the huge challenge of how to construct a cognitive theory that includes itself. This theory must not only explain a biological system but also explain the theory itself. This is indeed a very difficult task.

To nurture such a scientific marvel, European soil is clearly more suitable than American soil. The founder of second-order cybernetics, Heinz von Foerster, is the nephew of the famous linguist, mathematician, and philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein. He was influenced by the Vienna Circle from a young age and possesses profound foundations in philosophy, science, and art. After World War II, he immigrated to the United States and, by chance, participated in the early discussions on cybernetics conducted by top American scientists like Wiener, von Neumann, and Shannon. Heinz keenly recognized the deep implications of the feedback concept in cybernetics—a mathematical model distinct from classical scientific theories. Classical scientific theories rely on some axiomatic assumptions and then deduce conclusions step by step. This theoretical structure is directly related to the objectivist perspective of classical science. The feedback concept, however, breaks free from this linear causality: in a circular theoretical system, each link is both cause and effect, mutually defining, with nothing serving as the foundation for everything else. While many American scientists began modeling the external world as complex systems along the lines of feedback thinking, Heinz and other second-order cybernetics scholars began using feedback thinking to model the observer.

In the view of second-order cybernetics, the organism and the environment are inseparable. The organism and the environment together form a two-dimensional feedback loop, and the reality perceived by the organism is the "computational" result of this feedback loop. This process was depicted by Heinz as shown in the following diagram.

![Figure 13. The Two-Dimensional Cognitive Model of Second-Order Cybernetics](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image15.png)

*Figure 13. The Two-Dimensional Cognitive Model of Second-Order Cybernetics*

In the figure above, the black squares represent neurons, which have some local connections between them. SS represents the sensory interface (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body), and MS represents the behavioral interface (muscle control). The influence generated by MS is fed back to the SS interface via the external world. NP (Neuropituitary) represents neurotransmitters, the various chemical molecules secreted by nerve endings. These chemical molecules can alter the signal transmission efficiency between brain neurons (syn), thereby accomplishing learning functions. That is, the NP mechanism can gradually change the behavior of the neural network (i.e., the Hebbian learning rule of neurons) as the organism adapts to the environment.

If we curl and connect the two feedback loops (horizontal and vertical) in the above figure, we can form the following graphic:

![Figure 14. The Two-Dimensional Cognitive Model of Second-Order Cybernetics "Closed" into a Loop](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image16.png)

*Figure 14. The Two-Dimensional Cognitive Model of Second-Order Cybernetics "Closed" into a Loop*

This strange-looking doughnut is the basic model of the observer in second-order cybernetics. The two feedback loops of this doughnut, one is responsible for generating the phenomenal world we perceive, and the other is responsible for generating and altering this perceptual capacity.

Of course, such a model still faces many questions: How can such a simple feedback loop produce such a complex and colorful world experience? If an organism is such a feedback loop, what is its relationship with other organisms it perceives in the world? If this organism perishes, does the world also perish? Can a robot also imitate this process and become a true organism? To answer these thorny questions, we need to draw upon the concept of the "Seven Abodes of Consciousness" (satta viññāṇa-ṭṭhitiyo) in the Buddhism Dhamma, i.e., the seven steady states of awareness. The Buddha said that complete knowledge and non-attachment to these seven "steady states of awareness" constitute the path to ultimate liberation on the far shore.
