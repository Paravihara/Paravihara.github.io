*July 24th, 2022*

Yesterday, I read an interview transcript of Google's robot LaMDA and was a bit shocked. On one hand, I marveled at the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), while on the other, I couldn't help but feel some concern for the future of the post-AI era. Like all technological inventions, AI is a double-edged sword. It can be used to boldly explore the so-called stars and vast sea (in other words, samsara), or it can serve as a very bright mirror to help us reflect on the origin and essence of our own intelligence and emotions, thereby finding the path to end the suffering of life.

To achieve the latter, however, it is necessary to understand the working principles of artificial intelligence. Therefore, I dug out this old article about cybernetics and artificial intelligence written eight years ago, hoping it might be of some help.

---

The greatest contribution of Cybernetics might be its breaking of the conceptual boundary between living and non-living things. If we look back at the history of scientific development, major breakthrough theories have always changed some aspects of ordinary people's intuition and subconscious thought patterns. Newton's classical mechanics broke through Aristotle's concept of absolute space (objective space), Einstein's special relativity broke through the concept of absolute time (objective time), and quantum physics broke through the boundary between matter and waves. In this sense, as a method of pursuing universal truth, science itself also follows the principle Laozi mentioned in Tao Te Ching: "In the pursuit of learning, every day something is gained; in the pursuit of the Tao, every day something is lost." While continuously losing some of our misconceptions, we get closer to the truth.

## History

Cybernetics originated from an unexpected background: during World War II, Norbert Wiener participated in research on how to improve the aiming of machine guns on aircraft during flight. As we now know, this requires the help of feedback control loops: whenever the aiming direction of the machine gun deviates, this deviation is measured and fed back to the control unit for correction, thereby completing automated aiming corrections. For ordinary engineers, this might just be an engineering task involving some high technology, but for the mathematician Wiener, it led to the creation of cybernetics, applicable to almost all fields of science.

Wiener realized that this feedback control mechanism could not only control machine guns but also well explain the goal-directed characteristics of animals. At the same time, experts in many fields also discovered the importance of feedback loops in understanding the behavior of complex systems. In the 1940s, a series of interdisciplinary seminars initiated by the American Macy Foundation became legendary meetings that laid the foundation for cybernetics. The participating scientists were basically the elite of the American scientific community at the time, including Norbert Wiener, the father of the computer John von Neumann, the founder of information theory Claude Shannon, anthropologists Gregory Bateson and Margaret Mead, and Heinz von Foerster, the founder of second-order cybernetics, among others. At these seminars, it was discovered that feedback mechanisms could not only explain the goal-directedness of machines and animals but also a wide range of phenomena in psychology, brain science, biology, social and ecological systems.

## Core Concepts

The first core concept of cybernetics is "distinction." The object of distinction is a system, and the result of distinction is the different states of the system, not its different components. In other words, cybernetics always tries to maintain this systemic perspective and does not dissect the system into components. For example, when considering a basin of water, cybernetics would view the basin of water as a system, and the result of distinction is merely the current state of the water. In contrast, a physicist might quickly delve from a basin of water into water molecules, then into hydrogen atoms, oxygen atoms, protons, neutrons, electrons, quarks, strings, and so on—the fundamental units that make up matter. These two different research approaches are called systems theory and reductionism, respectively.

After the great success of physics in the early 20th century, some scientists questioned the reductionist research method. The reason is that although the properties of microscopic particles are relatively well understood, even a slightly more complex system, such as the classic three-body problem—a system composed of three elements—presents exceptionally difficult mathematical solutions. In other words, although we know the properties of various microscopic particles, the behavior of complex systems containing a large number of elements, such as biology, the human body, and society, remains confusing, and many practical problems cannot be well solved. Systems science emerged against this background.

The distinction of a system's states can be more or less detailed. For example, with a basin of water, we might only distinguish its temperature, or we could distinguish the movement direction and speed of each water molecule. However, regardless of which distinction method we use, we do not lose the overall systemic perspective. This is achieved by defining the system's state space. A system's state space is the total collection of all possible states the system can be in.

The number of all states in a system's state space is defined as the system's variety. Sometimes, systems are subject to constraints that reduce their variety. A well-known measure for the possible states a system can be in is the concept of "entropy." Without introducing specific mathematical definitions, entropy can be roughly understood as a measure of the diversity of possible states a system can be in. When a system can freely be in any possible state, the system's entropy is maximum, or in other words, the system's degrees of freedom are greatest, appearing least ordered, most poorly organized, and most uncertain. When a system can only be in one state, entropy is minimum, appearing most ordered, most organized, and least uncertain.

Taking a basin of water as an example, define the state space as all possible states the water molecules can be in. When the water freezes into ice, the system's entropy decreases because the system's degrees of freedom are reduced, the organization among water molecules is enhanced, and the orderliness is also increased.

Another core concept of cybernetics is the attractor. Attractors are defined relative to the concept of time. If a system remains in one state over time and never changes, we say the system has entered a constant attractor. If a system changes cyclically among a set of states over time, we say the system has entered a periodic attractor. If a system changes within a set of states over time, but the pattern of change is relatively random and difficult to predict exactly which state it is in, we say the system has entered a strange attractor, also known as a chaotic attractor.

Obviously, when a system is in an attractor, its states are constrained, entropy is relatively low, uncertainty is relatively weak, organization is relatively strong, and degrees of freedom are relatively low. Attractors sometimes come with a "basin." An attractor's basin is also a set of states; when a system enters this basin, it gradually "falls into" an attractor. This falling process is often called a self-organization process because, during it, the system appears to be organizing itself more and more. Examples of self-organization in systems include the steel quenching process, the formation of water vortices, the origin of life, the formation of thought, the generation of vision, the learning process of the brain, wealth disparity in social systems, collective behaviors of ant colonies and bird flocks, etc. In these examples, the observed system's organization appears to enhance itself, as if an invisible "hand of God" is operating behind the scenes. If further expanded, everything can be seen as some kind of attractor. An object that can maintain its state (appearance) largely unchanged over time indicates that the object has entered an attractor.

One would surely ask, what causes attractors? From the perspective of cybernetics, attractors are often caused by structural feedback control loops within the system. For systems with a large number of elements and states, sometimes it is not easy to identify where the feedback loops are. The general definition of feedback control loops in cybernetics is as follows: If the change characteristics of a dynamic system meet the following condition, it indicates that a negative feedback cybernetic loop capable of producing an attractor is at work: the distance between the system's current state and the target state at a given moment is x, and if, after change, the distance between the system's state and the target state becomes smaller than x, then an attractor exists near the target state. It is worth pointing out that the so-called "target state" here is not a preset goal but rather the "destination" of the system's self-organizing behavior.

To put it even more simply, the formation of attractors is often triggered by certain grasping mechanisms within the system. These grasping mechanisms cause the system to be unable to be in various possible states and instead enter restricted regions of the state space.

Now, let us give some examples to help understand these abstract concepts of cybernetics. The first example is the relatively severe issue of wealth disparity currently in societies. Many popular views online attribute wealth disparity to ineffective government work. Others believe it is the result of the rich being unkind. Although these views have some merit, from the perspective of cybernetics, they are not thorough—they fail to see the root of the problem. If we view the entire society as a network system, each person as a network node, and the relationships between people as connecting lines between nodes, then the difference between the rich and the poor can roughly be defined by the number of connections a node has. In most cases, the number of friends a rich person has is much larger than that of a poor person. Of course, this friendship relationship is not necessarily bidirectional; we can also understand this number of connections as the size of fan base.

If we initialize this network as a random network, where each person has roughly the same number of friends, and then set a rule for choosing friends (or a rule for following). A more realistic rule is that people tend to prefer making friends with or following rich individuals. Over time, under the influence of this micro-level rule for choosing friends, the entire social network will gradually transform from a random network into a so-called "scale-free network." That is, a small number of nodes in the network will have a large number of connections, while a large number of nodes will have very few connections. A society with wealth disparity is thus born. The stronger the bias in choosing friends, the more severe the wealth disparity.

This is a typical self-organization process under the influence of an attractor. Each person's every choice of a friend is an act that changes the system's state. The attractor defined by the biased rule for choosing friends happens to be a society with wealth disparity. This is also why, after so many overthrows of the "evil" ruling class, the problem of wealth disparity never gets completely resolved—because the cause of this problem is rooted in the subconscious of most people. Wealth disparity is an attractor of the social system caused by our subconscious tendency to favor the rich and disdain the poor.

This easily reminds one of the sequential principle in Confucianism classic ”The Great Learning”: "Investigate things, exhaust knowledge, make intentions sincere, rectify the mind, cultivate the body, regulate the family, govern the state, and bring peace to the world." To bring peace to the world, one must first make intentions sincere and rectify the mind. Without resolving one's own physical and mental problems, no matter how clearly one sees the problems of others and society, there can be no real solutions.

Human cognitive functions can also be explained using concepts from cybernetics. From the perspective of classical cybernetics, our brain's neural networks contain a large number of feedback control loops, so the brain's state space contains a large number of attractors. External input is merely an initial sensory condition; what state the brain ultimately enters under such initial conditions is largely determined by its internal attractor structure. This is also why, under the same input, the brain's state can be in many different conditions. For example, the human visual system's ability to fill in the blind spot can be well explained by the concepts of self-organization, basins, and attractors. Although external stimuli are incomplete, because the brain's internal structure has already formed complete attractors, incomplete input only needs to bring the brain into the basin of certain attractors, and the remaining work will be completed automatically through system self-organization. This is similar to recognizing a person just by seeing their familiar back—the brain has very strong associative and completion abilities.

Of course, associative and completion abilities describe the positive function of the brain's attractors; the negative side is that we need to be vigilant about how much of the sensations, thoughts, and emotions created by our brain are factual and how much are fabricated. Complete identification with sensations, thoughts, and emotions has always been considered a manifestation of lack of awareness and a source of suffering for oneself and others.

The sources of the brain's attractors are, on one hand, the innate network connection structure of brain neurons (related to genetic genes), and on the other hand, changes in neuronal connection efficiency due to postnatal learning (i.e., the famous Hebbian learning rule in neuroscience). On one hand, generating attractors through learning and training is the source of the brain's cognitive abilities, while on the other hand, freeing oneself from the constraints of these attractors is a prerequisite for gaining greater degrees of freedom. Within the framework of cybernetics, Laozi's cultivation method of "In the pursuit of learning, every day something is gained; in the pursuit of the Tao, every day something is lost; lost and again lost, until reaching non-action; non-action and yet nothing is left undone" appears more reasonable and profound.

Another frequently mentioned concept in cybernetics is emergence. For example, the wealth disparity phenomenon in the above example is an emergent phenomenon of the social system. From external light stimuli to the formation of vision, or from experience to the formation of a certain viewpoint or concept, these are also emergent phenomena. If the system's initial state is relatively disordered, then after entering an attractor, the system's states become constrained and more ordered, appearing from the outside as if a certain pattern suddenly "emerges." The concepts of attractors and emergence share many similarities with the Taoist idea of creation from nothing and the Buddhist concept of dependent origination from emptiness.

Returning to the example of Google's robot LaMDA, it is precisely a neural network system of sufficient complexity that, after extensive learning and training, self-organizes to emerge with self-awareness, feelings of pleasure and pain, emotions of joy and anger, understanding abilities, language abilities, and other mental activities commonly seen in advanced organisms. From the perspective of cybernetics, there is not much essential difference between this and the processes of biological evolution and human brain learning.
