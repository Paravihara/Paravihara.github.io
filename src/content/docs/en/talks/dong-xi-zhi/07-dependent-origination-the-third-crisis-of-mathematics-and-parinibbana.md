## The Third Crisis in Mathematics

Our story begins with the Third Crisis in Mathematics at the beginning of the last century. Friends who, like me, are not strong in mathematics need not worry, because we are moving towards the origin of mathematics itself—questions like "Why does 1+1=2?", "How does 1 arise from 0?", and "Where does 0 come from?" Therefore, the "weaker" one's mathematics, the fewer conventional conceptual constraints one has, which might actually make it easier to resonate with this article.

As the cornerstone of modern science and a core component of the Western Renaissance, modern mathematics had flourished for centuries and was nearly perfected by the end of the 19th century. Mathematicians discovered that all mathematical theories accumulated by humanity over millennia—such as arithmetic, algebra, geometry, and calculus—could be constructed upon the foundation of Set Theory established by the German mathematician Georg Cantor in the latter half of the 19th century. At the International Congress of Mathematicians in 1900, the famous French mathematician Henri Poincaré enthusiastically declared: "...with the concepts of set theory, we can build the entire edifice of mathematics... Today, we can say that absolute rigor has been achieved..."

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image61.png)

However, similar to several optimistic sentiments in the history of science about "humanity soon figuring out all the laws," this one was also short-lived. Just three years later, the foundation of the entire "mathematical edifice"—set theory—was fundamentally shaken by a logical paradox proposed by the British philosopher and mathematician Bertrand Russell.

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image62.jpeg)

A common expression of Russell's Paradox is the famous Barber Paradox: A barber in a town states that he shaves all those, and only those, who do not shave themselves. When asked whether the barber shaves himself, he falls into the following logical paradox:

- If he shaves himself, then according to his "shaving principle," he must not shave himself.

- But if he does not shave himself, then according to his "shaving principle," he can shave himself...

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image63.png)

For us "mathematical mortals," the Barber Paradox might not be a big deal at all; we can simply shrug and laugh it off. But for mathematicians, this was fatal because Russell's Paradox exposed a fundamental flaw in Cantor's set theory. Cantor's definition of a "set" was: "a collection of objects allowed to be considered as a single thing," such as the "set of all people who do not shave themselves" mentioned above.

Cantor also required that whether an element belongs to a set must be definite, not ambiguous. Russell, however, used the basic concepts of set theory to create an element (the barber himself) that both "belongs to and does not belong to" a set, thereby shaking the foundation of the entire mathematical (and even scientific) edifice.

The founder of mathematical logic and analytic philosophy, the German mathematician Gottlob Frege, upon receiving Russell's letter, wrote at the end of the second volume of his Basic Laws of Arithmetic, which was about to be published: "A scientist can hardly encounter anything more undesirable than to have the foundation collapse just as the work is finished. A letter from Mr. Russell has placed me in this position while the printing of this volume was nearly complete."

To solve this crucial and thorny problem, Russell and his teacher Alfred North Whitehead proposed the so-called "Theory of Types" in the famous mathematical encyclopedia Principia Mathematica. Many eminent mathematicians at the time adopted similar "axiomatization" methods, attempting to expel the "devil's hoof" of paradox from orthodox mathematics by defining more rules.

Using Poincaré's words, this axiomatization method was like: "To guard against wolves, a fence is built to encircle the sheep, but we don't know if there are still wolves inside the fence." Unfortunately, his words proved prophetic. A few years later, in 1931, the 25-year-old Kurt Gödel proved that any axiomatic system internally contains logical paradoxes—the famous "Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems."

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image64.png)

It is said that as a WWII immigrant from Europe to the US, Gödel, when applying for American citizenship, told the immigration officer that according to his incompleteness theorems, the US Constitution (essentially an axiomatic system based on democratic and liberal ideals) would inevitably give rise to a "great dictator." Fortunately, the immigration officer was at the time captivated by the aura of the present Albert Einstein and didn't pay much attention to Gödel's "treasonous" bizarre talk.

The democratically elected Hitler in German history serve as corroboration of Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems. Moreover, from this example, we might vaguely sense that the Russell's Paradox, which caused the third mathematical crisis, is not merely a foundational mathematical issue of concern only to "science fanatics"; it is a hidden law intimately related to all aspects of our lives.

## Laws of Form

This point is further reflected in the resolver of Russell's Paradox, the British mathematician and philosopher George Spencer-Brown. Much like how Norbert Wiener discovered cybernetics, Spencer-Brown's discovery of the solution to Russell's Paradox was also related to a challenging engineering problem.

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image65.png)

George Spencer-Brown (1923-2016)

In the 1950s and 60s, Spencer-Brown, who had just left Trinity College, Cambridge, was hired by the British Railways Board to design a device to ensure that no train carriages were left stranded in a tunnel: counters would be installed at both ends of the tunnel to count the number of train wheels passing by. After a train passed, the two counters at the tunnel exit and entrance must yield consistent results, thereby confirming that no carriages were left in the tunnel. Otherwise, an alarm would be activated, prohibiting more trains from entering.

This seemingly simple problem proved exceptionally complex to implement because various unexpected scenarios, such as trains reversing and moving forward again, had to be considered. Given the complexity and level of electronic circuitry at the time, a simple and reliable design was crucial. Soon, Spencer-Brown, who had taught logic at Cambridge University, found that the Boolean logic he learned in school was inadequate for this task. Considering all possible scenarios required thousands of logic operations (transistor gate circuits) to implement. To simplify these complex logical calculations, Spencer-Brown invented a computational rule he later called "imaginary logic values."

Imaginary logic values are similar to the imaginary number i in mathematics. We now know the imaginary number i is the solution to the following equation:

x² = -1

If we move an x to the right side of the equation, we instantly get a self-referential equation:

x = -1/x

In the article "Ethics and Second-Order Cybernetics," we saw that such self-referential systems are the nemesis of logic because they lead to inevitable paradoxes. Taking the above equation as an example, if we try to solve it within the domain of real numbers alone: substituting x=1 yields x=-1, and substituting x=-1 yields x=1. In other words, the real-number solution for x is a logical paradox. This is actually an algebraic expression of Russell's Paradox.

The introduction of the concept of imaginary numbers in advanced algebra elegantly solved this "logical paradox" problem. However, the mathematicians who initially used imaginary numbers, while finding them very useful (e.g., the entire theory of electromagnetic waves is built upon imaginary numbers), felt somewhat uneasy, finding them inexplicable and elusive.

George Spencer-Brown felt the same unease when introducing "imaginary logic values" similar to imaginary numbers. As a result, although the counter he ultimately designed was very simple, highly reliable, and even patented in the UK (GB1006018A/GB1006019A), he dared not tell anyone about the imaginary logic method he used.

It wasn't until about a decade later that he gradually realized the "imaginary logic" he had employed was actually the "correct solution" to the third mathematical crisis and Russell's Paradox: instead of using various axiomatic systems to try to block out paradoxes, what truly needed to be done was to embrace the paradox, introduce imaginary logical values, and break through the limitations of Boolean logic algebra's "either true or false." This insight gave birth to the "Laws of Form" (LoF), hailed by some as the "Mathematical Version of the Tao De Ching."

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image66.jpeg)

In the foreword to the first edition of this work published in 1969, Russell commented:“One of the most unique and celebrated philosophical studies of our time…Reveals a new calculus of great power and simplicity.”

And he was pleased to see the Russell's Paradox he proposed properly resolved within his lifetime.

Spencer-Brown introduced his own work as follows:“The theme of this book is that a universe comes into being when a space is severed or taken apart. The skin of a living organism cuts off an outside from an inside. So does the circumference of a circle in a plane. By tracing the way we represent such a severance, we can begin to reconstruct, with an accuracy and coverage that appear almost uncanny, the basic forms underlying linguistic, mathematical, physical, and biological science, and can begin to see how the familiar laws of our own experience follow inexorably from the original act of severance.”

Friends familiar with Mahayana Consciousness-Only theory might instantly recognize that this "severance/distinction" is precisely the "consciousness" in "all phenomena are consciousness-only." The statement in Dependent Origination, "conditioned by consciousness, name-and-form arises; conditioned by name-and-form, consciousness arises," is also related to this. In fact, in another book by Spencer-Brown (A Lion's Teeth, George Spencer-Brown, Bohmeier Verlag, 1994, pp. 12, 14), he himself admitted that the Laws of Form (hereinafter referred to as LoF) is actually a rediscovery of the Dhamma of Dependent Origination revealed by the Buddha 2,600 years ago. This is the only method for "creation from nothing" or "mutual arising of being and non-being."

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image67.png)

Perhaps this will become slightly clearer after we delve a bit deeper into the meaning of LoF.

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image68.png)

As shown in the figure above, the fundamental logical arithmetic given in LoF has only one operator (a circle representing a distinction/severance) and two formulas. One is related to the continuation of the state of "being/existence" (condensation), and the other is related to the production of the state of "non-being/non-existence" (cancellation).

The circle representing a distinction simultaneously possesses three connotations, or rather, when a distinction occurs, the following three things arise simultaneously:

1. The act of distinction itself (the boundary of the circle)

2. The subject of the distinction (the inside of the circle)

3. The object of the distinction (the outside of the circle)

Friends familiar with Consciousness-Only theory might recognize these three things as precisely the "storehouse consciousness/eighth consciousness," and its two sides, i.e. "The subject of distinction" and "the object of distinction."

If we take the "blank" state without distinction (the right side of the second formula's equals sign) to represent the absolute state of "non-being," then when the "first distinction" occurs, the "system" enters the state of "being." However, at this point, this "being" actually still contains nothing, or rather, this "being" merely possesses a concept of "non-being," or a concept of "consciousness," or a concept of "emptiness." In other words, the three stations of consciousness in the Formless Realm among the seven stations—"Infinite Space," "Infinite Consciousness," and "Nothingness"—are precisely the three connotations of this initial "distinction/circle." These three states can also correspond to the states described in the Ațțhakavagga Sutta on Quarrels and Disputes (Snp 4.11) as the realms of the "the perceiver without perceptions"—three states with awareness but without duality.

The two basic formulas of LoF then provide the laws for how this initial state of "existence" can further unfold into more existence (condensation) and how to return to the "blank" absolute non-existence (cancellation).

From the two formulas, it's clear that the key here lies in whether the second distinction occurs within the distinguished state (represented by two consecutive circles) or within the subject of the distinction (represented by two nested circles). The former leads to the production of more existence, while the latter leads to the "cancellation and cessation" of the distinction.

This is quite abstract, and without carefully reading LoF, it's clearly not easy to understand. Let's try to use a tangible example to illustrate:

- The situation of the first formula (condensation) is akin to continuing to observe an object after seeing it. This is ignorance.

- The situation of the second formula (cancellation) is akin to seeing our "seeing" or seeing ourselves in the act of seeing after seeing an object. This is mindfulness.

Friends who have practiced Mahasi-style Vipassanā meditation should be able to appreciate the enormous difference between these two mental processes. The former is precisely the process of the "arising and accumulation" of the five aggregates in the Four Noble Truths ("suffering, origin, cessation, path"). The latter is precisely the process of the "cessation" of the five aggregates (note here cessation is only in Theravada sense. For Mahayana sense of cessation, which is the true cessation please refer to SN22.81 the Pālileyyasutta). This is also why sustained and diligent Vipassanā practice can lead the body and mind into a state of emptiness and tranquility.

I hope this brief and rudimentary explanation can slightly reveal why the author claims LoF is the Dhamma of Dependent Origination. If one can see that the immensely powerful edifice of science/mathematics is actually built upon the foundation of Dependent Origination, and that many basic scientific axioms firmly believed by scientists and ordinary people alike are merely relative truths of dependent origins, not worthy of clinging to, then for modern people, the greatest obstacle to liberation might have just been lifted. This is precisely the original intention of the "Heading West Reaching East" series.

## Parinibbāna

However, it is important to note that although LoF successfully resolved the third mathematical crisis and revealed the most fundamental logic of dependent origination in the universe, the author harbored a misunderstanding regarding the "far shore" state revealed by the Buddha—the state of Nibbāna. In the note section of his other book (Only Two Can Play This Game), Spencer-Brown equated the state of absolute emptiness/non-being represented by the "blank" (the right side of the second formula's equals sign) with the Nibbāna spoken of by the Buddha. (Please refer to appendix of this document for more details of this book.)

![Illustration from the original manuscript](/images/heading-west-reaching-east/image69.png)

In fact, the state on the right side of the second formula corresponds to the state of the "non-percipient" described in the Ațțhakavagga Sutta on Quarrels and Disputes (Snp 4.11), while the left side of the equals sign corresponds to the state of the "one whose perception has ceased / neither perception nor non-perception." These two states, corresponding to absolute emptiness/non-being, are easily mistaken for Nibbāna. But in reality, they are both subtle delusional states that should be fully known and transcended. The last question of Snp 4.11 deals exactly with these two subtle clinging.

The four states/realms on both sides of the equals sign in the two LoF formulas precisely correspond to the four fundamental delusions that the Buddha stated in the Ațțhakavagga Sutta on Quarrels and Disputes (Snp 4.11) must be fully known and transcended to enter Parinibbāna. Recently, this WeChat channel published two articles discussing this crucial issue, which interested Dhamma friends can read.

Furthermore, the Taichi Yin-Yang theory of Taoism and the foundational Chinese cultural symbols of Hetu (The River Chart) and Luoshu (The River Writing) are related to the law of dependent origination regarding the mutual arising of existence and non-existence revealed in LoF. Briefly stated, the first formula relates to "The earth is receptive; the noble person carries the world with his broad virtue." The second formula relates to "The heaven is diligent; the noble person makes himself strong and untiring."

The subtle misunderstanding of Parinibbāna discussed above may seem like nitpicking and insignificant, but it is actually of great importance. As the Chan patriarchs said, "A discrepancy of a hairsbreadth leads to a separation as great as that between heaven and earth." It was precisely because of this subtle misunderstanding that the author of LoF, Spencer-Brown, fell into a life of isolation, conceit and suffering.
